## Q1 (D1). How often do patients come too late with caries that could have been treated earlier?

**Dentist:**
This is actually a very common situation. Almost every day, patients come to the clinic with advanced cases of dental caries, even though the condition could have been detected and treated at an earlier stage before complications developed.

Unfortunately, many people neglect preventive dental examinations and visit a dentist only when they experience discomfort, such as pain when consuming hot or cold food or drinks, pain when biting, or other unpleasant symptoms.

If nothing bothers them, they often do not attend regular checkups or professional dental hygiene appointments. As a result, dental problems are not detected in time.

Therefore, regular preventive examinations are very important for identifying caries at an early stage and preventing complications.

## Q2 (D2). Would a patient's photo with marked suspicious regions be useful before a visit?

**Dentist:**
When a patient visits the clinic, I perform a visual examination of the oral cavity. Dentists also generally use radiographic examinations, including 3D imaging, to identify problematic teeth.

If there were an opportunity to see photographs of the patient's teeth with suspicious areas highlighted before the appointment, it would probably be convenient.

Such information could help the dentist understand possible problems in advance and prepare for the patient's visit.

However, a proper clinical examination and additional diagnostic procedures would still be necessary.

## Q4 (D4). Which photo angles are needed to see most caries?

**Dentist:**
It depends on which part of the tooth is affected and on which surface the caries is located.

During an initial examination, we usually follow a dental photography protocol that includes several types of photographs.

These include photographs of the patient's smile, frontal photographs taken using cheek retractors, and photographs taken with intraoral mirrors.

We also photograph the upper and lower dental arches from the occlusal, or chewing-surface, view. Special intraoral mirrors are used for these photographs.

Photographs of the buccal surfaces are less commonly taken in this format and are more frequently used in orthodontics.

For a basic dental photography protocol, the most important views include:
- A photograph of the patient's smile.
- A frontal photograph using cheek retractors.
- An occlusal photograph of the upper dental arch using an intraoral mirror.
- An occlusal photograph of the lower dental arch using an intraoral mirror.

Therefore, a single photograph is not sufficient to evaluate all tooth surfaces. The required angles depend on the location of the suspected problem.

## Q5 (D5). What is worse for a screening tool: missing caries or generating a false alarm?

**Dentist:**
Missing caries is definitely worse.

It is better to be overly cautious and draw attention to a potential problem than to fail to detect a condition that could eventually lead to complications.

If the system indicates possible caries, the patient can visit a dentist for further examination.

## Q6 (D6). What wording should the service use so that patients do not treat the result as a diagnosis?

**Dentist:**
This situation is similar to cases where patients call a dental clinic and try to obtain a diagnosis from the receptionist over the phone.

They describe their symptoms, ask how much treatment will cost, and want to know what is wrong with their teeth. However, a receptionist cannot determine a diagnosis based only on the patient's description.

Even an experienced dental professional needs to conduct a proper visual examination. Additional diagnostic procedures may also be required, such as a targeted dental X-ray or, ideally when clinically indicated, a 3D scan.

Therefore, the result of such a system should not be presented as a final diagnosis.

The platform could display a warning explaining that the analysis is preliminary and that only a qualified dentist can establish an accurate diagnosis after an examination.

Patients should not rely entirely on the information provided by the service. The system may suggest a possible problem, but a dental examination is required to confirm or rule out the condition.

**Interviewer:**
So we should clearly state that the result is not a medical diagnosis?

**Dentist:**
Yes, exactly. It is only an assumption, not a final diagnosis. In any case, the patient needs to be examined by a dentist.
